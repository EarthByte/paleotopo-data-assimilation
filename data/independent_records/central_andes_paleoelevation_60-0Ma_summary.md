# Central Andes independent paleoelevation records, 60–0 Ma

The independent record places the central Andean plateau low through the Oligocene, with high elevation arriving diachronously in the Miocene. Clumped-isotope paleosols in the Bolivian Eastern Cordillera give 0–1.5 km at 29–24 Ma and about 2.5 km by 20–15 Ma (Leier et al. 2013). Leaf physiognomy puts the Jakokkota flora at 590–1610 ± 1000 m at 10.7 Ma, where the surface stands at 3940 m today (Gregory-Wodzicki et al. 1998). In the northern Altiplano, carbonate δ18O and Δ47 record 2.5–3.5 km of rise between about 10.3 and 6.8 Ma (Garzione et al. 2006; Ghosh et al. 2006). The northernmost Altiplano in Peru stood at 0.9–2.1 km until about 9 Ma and reached about 4 km by 5.4 Ma (Kar et al. 2016). The flanks rose earlier. Volcanic-glass δD puts a Western Cordillera basin in southern Peru at its modern elevation by 16 Ma (Saylor & Horton 2014), and the Puna has been near 4 km since about 36 Ma (Canavan et al. 2014) and near its present elevation since at least 10 Ma (Carrapa et al. 2014).

The main debate sets punctuated uplift driven by lithospheric removal against slow, steady uplift driven by shortening. The slow-and-steady side argues that part of the late Miocene δ18O and temperature shift is a climate response to crossing threshold elevations (Ehlers & Poulsen 2009). The Puna glass record is also contested, because clumped-isotope thermometry there was reset during burial (Quade et al. 2015).

The most reliable rows are the Δ47-based Leier and Kar estimates, the Jakokkota flora and the Saylor & Horton δD record. The near-modern and relative rows carry no absolute number and should be compared against the present-day DEM at each site.
