# Southern Tibet independent paleoelevation records, 60–0 Ma

The southern Lhasa block was high for most of the Cenozoic. Clumped isotopes from the Penbo basin give 4.4 (+1.3/−1.7) km for the Paleocene–Eocene Nianbo Formation and 4.1 (+1.2/−1.6) km for the Eocene Pana Formation (Ingalls et al. 2018). Oxygen isotopes from the nearby Linzhou basin give about 4.5 ± 0.4 km at about 56 Ma (Ding et al. 2014). The Oiyug basin records 4.1 km from the late Oligocene to the mid-Miocene, 5.1 km at about 15 Ma and 5.5 km at about 5 Ma, so the present 4.3 km allows for late Cenozoic extensional lowering (Currie et al. 2016). CLAMP on the Namling flora gives 4689 ± 895 m at 15 Ma, close to the modern 4600 m (Spicer et al. 2003). The Himalaya rose later than the Lhasa block, from about 1 km at 56 Ma to about 2.3 km at 21–19 Ma (Ding et al. 2017), and stood at 5 km or more around Everest by the late Early Miocene (Gébelin et al. 2013). Central Tibet stood at 4.5–5 km at Nima by 26 Ma (DeCelles et al. 2007).

Most of the debate concerns the Eocene and the plateau interior. Botsyun et al. (2019) argue that Eocene δ18O paleoaltimetry is biased high and that the plateau stood below 3 km. At Lunpola, the claim of more than 4 km since 35 Ma (Rowley & Currie 2006) conflicts with palm fossils that put the valley floor below 2.3 km (Su et al. 2019). Fang et al. (2020) reconcile the two with a revised chronology: below 2.3 km at 39.5 Ma and 3.5–4.5 km by about 26 Ma. Late Eocene foraminifera at Gerze suggest the northern Lhasa block lay near sea level (Wei et al. 2016).

The most reliable rows are the clumped-isotope Penbo and Oiyug estimates, the Namling CLAMP estimate, the multiproxy Oiyug estimates and Nima.
